Slim Diamond

A wireless, low-profile remake of the handwired Diamond (1333+2 per half). The layout and goals are mine; the PCB, the printed case and every check were generated as code by Claude, Anthropic's model, working in Claude Code.

One PCB, two halves

The reversible trick

The left half puts its parts on the back face; the right half is the same board flipped, with parts on the front. Flipped, the module's pins land mirrored: where the left half has D0, D1 and D2, the right half has VBUS, GND and 3V3. Solder jumpers route those three to their keys, and you bridge only the set on the face without the module.

The module's underside pads (battery, the 12th key's pin, reset) are reached through plated holes filled with solder from the other face, so the whole board stays iron-friendly. The reset pad sits exactly under the module's GND pad on the opposite mount, so each half's reset hole gets its own net and only reaches that half's button.

One board, either module

XIAO or Super52840

Both modules have the same 14 edge pins on the same nRF52840 pins, so the layout, case, plate and firmware are shared. Underneath they differ. Their battery pads sit in the same two spots but swapped, so a battery selector on the switch face picks which hole is +: bridge the row labelled +MID or +USB. The Super52840's pads were measured from photos against the 2.54 mm pin pitch.

Toolchain

Everything is a script

Checks

What gets checked

Every part that could collide is modelled, and the fit check fails on anything above 0 mm³. Some checks cover how the keyboard is assembled and used, not only where parts sit:

A USB-C cable seats fully

A recess the size of the largest Type-C overmold (12.35 × 6.5 mm) reaches the port, and the PCB edge is notched in front of it. The check plugs in a cable of that size.

The PCB drops straight in

The power switch's lever reaches past the PCB edge, so it slides down a slot open to the top. The slot widens outwards so a fingernail reaches the lever. The check sweeps the lever along the drop-in path.

Room for tolerances

0.5 mm around the PCB and plate covers JLCPCB's ±0.2 mm outline tolerance plus print shrinkage.

One magnet throughout

Thirteen 8 × 1.5 mm N52 discs per half: six along the seam and seven underneath, including three circular stand contacts. Each pocket keeps 0.5 mm of plastic all round, measured against the printed base. The larger battery raises the PCB and case by 1.8 mm without changing the PCB outline or wiring.

Cyberdeck

A dock for a phone stand

A phone on a Ugreen folding stand is the computer and the screen. The dock is one printed part that holds both halves around it. It sits over the stand's base, which stays on the desk on its own feet and carries the phone; behind the dock the base is bare, its hinge half a millimetre off the halves and the spine. Without the stand, it is a sturdier, tented unibody.

The dock continues the halves' 45° bottom facets down to the desk, with 12° splay and 5.96° extra tent (12.5° total). Its middle is a front-opening drawer for a trackball, trackpad or solid cover, preserving continuous front facets and horizontal removal. At the back each insert extends only its top into a 45° hood over a matching sloped dock support, with 0.25 mm modeled normal clearance. USB relief and the hood recess remove material from the spine; old stiffness estimates predate those cuts. Test the printed dock before lap use.

Every magnet is an 8 × 1.5 mm N52 disc. Six wing magnets mate six of each half's seven floor magnets; six per side fit 2.4 mm drawer walls with 0.6 mm inner skins. Three insert magnets per side align through those skins, forming an attracting half → dock → insert chain. Five pairs attach the dock to the Ugreen base. There is no rear retention magnet: that space is reserved for USB and the power switch. The dock is 195.1 × 111.0 × 30.2 mm, 84.4 cm³, with six bump-ons. Real magnetic retention and adhesive strength still need testing.

Independent tenting

Use two stands, with three common discs glued to each tilting phone platform, not the base. The mirrored SVG templates place them 120° apart on a 44 mm circle around the estimated assembled centre of gravity. A clear flat 54 mm contact patch is required: the actual Ugreen platform remains unmeasured. Specified 2.0 mm bump-ons clear the platform by 0.6 mm. The estimated gravity projection remains inside the contact triangle through 35° of tilt, but this does not certify magnetic holding force or stand stability.

Pointing

A trackball or a trackpad, in the drawer

Both pointing devices retain their own nice!nano, 752025 250 mAh battery, power switch and reset. Three common side magnets per side use every other position in the halves' six-magnet row: 35.6 mm pitch on the inserts, 17.8 mm on the halves and dock. There is no floor or rear-wall retention magnet. The 2.2 mm bottom plates retain 0.7 mm above foot recesses. Magnet centres are 7.2 mm above the desk, or 16.381 mm docked with the standing plane at 9.181 mm. Regression checks cover alignment, side skins, equal spacing, horizontal removal, both standalone sides and seated USB clearance.

The trackball: a 34 mm ball and PMW3610 sensor, with two MX or Choc v1 hot-swap keys on one shared PCB. Populate only one socket family. Two shells provide the correct plate thickness: MX 1.5 mm, Choc 1.2 mm with its top 2.8 mm lower. Both share the ball/sensor positions and bottom plate. The nice!nano is supported below the PCB and wired to labelled pads, avoiding socket/pin-hole overlap. The trackpad: a 40 mm Cirque pad and two hand-wired Choc keys, flush with the halves.

Keys move 3.8 mm rearward on the trackball and 4 mm on the trackpad; the trackball controller moves 1.8 mm rearward onto shell-mounted ledges, staying in the shell while the battery/sensor floor lowers for service. A smaller upper bevel preserves the rear switch rim. The dock USB opening is 12.8 mm wide all the way through the underside, passing a normally oriented plug.

Print caveat: the top-down trackball shells need build-plate supports under the projecting rear hood. Inspect support access and sliced first layers, then test-print the region; physical validation remains outstanding. Interior views are cutaways, not disassembly instructions. The audit regressions cover screw seats, foot roofs, switch-lever travel, populated floor extraction and binary-STL topology.

Flex feasibility research: Leonardo uses routed rigid-PCB springs. Retain rigid boards and wires for this release; JLCPCB's 100 × 100 mm size tier alone does not establish flex pricing, stiffeners or bend durability.

USB-C, seated

The rear connection is shown with an illustrative 12.35 × 6.5 mm plug and cable. With the drawer empty, feed the plug normally through the dock's 12.8 mm upper rear passage, connect the module, then slide it in while feeding the cable. The lower passage stays narrower to protect the side-magnet skins. Real plug dimensions and bend radius still need checking; if the plug is too large, remove the module for charging instead.

Build guide

Soldering and assembly

The module, battery and battery socket all end up inside the case, under the PCB. Only the USB-C port and the power-switch lever show, through openings in the outer wall: a recess the size of a cable's plug in front of the port, and a slot open to the top for the lever. Push the lever with a fingernail. The module's LED and its own reset button face the case floor, so flash and test it before closing the case. A second reset button on the switch face, wired to the module's reset pad, works with the case closed: it sits in a cone in the plate, above the thumbs.

Everything is soldered with an iron: no paste, no hot air. Build one half at a time. Both halves use the same PCB, turned over for the right half.

The module is a Seeed XIAO nRF52840 or a NologoTech Super52840. The board takes either: a battery selector, set in step 1, matches it to your module. The other steps are the same for both.

Tools

  • Temperature-controlled iron with a small chisel tip (1.6–2 mm) for the module, sockets and JST tabs, and a fine conical tip for the power switch.
  • Leaded solder: about 330 °C.
  • Lead-free: about 360 °C.
  • Thin solder (0.5–0.6 mm), a flux pen, fine tweezers, kapton tape, isopropyl alcohol and a brush.
  • A multimeter with a continuity beep.
  • Optional: a magnifier or phone camera to inspect the small pads.

The two faces

Each board has a parts face and a switch face. The silkscreen tells you which is which:

Half Parts face (silkscreen) Switch face
Left LEFT: parts on this face the other one
Right RIGHT: parts on this face the other one
  • Parts face: the module, power switch, JST socket and the 12 hot-swap sockets. It faces the case floor.
  • Switch face: the key switches clip in here, the reset button is soldered here, and you bridge the jumpers and fill the module's holes from this side.

The module always lies flat on the parts face, with its pad side against the PCB and its components (USB-C, shield, reset button) pointing away from it, towards the case floor.

1. Bridge the D0–D2 jumpers and the battery selector (switch face)

On the switch face, inside the module's outline, there are three small pad pairs labelled D0, D1, D2, on the face without the module. Flux them and bridge each pair with a small blob of solder.

Next to them is the battery selector: two rows of two pad pairs, labelled +MID and +USB. The two modules have their battery pads in the same spots, swapped, and the row you bridge decides which battery hole is +:

Module Bridge both pairs in row
Seeed XIAO nRF52840 +MID (its + pad is the one nearer the module's middle)
Super52840 +USB (its B+ pad is the one nearer the USB end)

Bridge one row only. Both rows short the battery; the wrong row connects it backwards. Both halves get the same row.

Left: the left half's switch face. Right: the right half's switch face. Bridge the three D0–D2 pairs and one selector row, and later fill the three boxed hole groups (step 2).

Leave the jumpers on the other face open: they're on the parts face, under where the module will sit.

Check: each bridged pair beeps on the multimeter, and the unbridged selector row doesn't.

2. Solder the module (parts face)

  1. Pre-tin the module's underside pads that the boxed holes reach: the two battery pads (VBAT and GND on the XIAO, B+ and B- on the Super52840), the P0.09 pad (NFC1 on the XIAO, 009 on the Super52840) and RST. Flux each one and touch it with a lightly tinned iron, leaving a thin, flat coat. Wick off any bump: a blob lifts the module off its edge pads. In step 4 the solder fed into each hole then only has to melt into the coat that's already there.
  2. Flux the 14 edge pads. Lay the module flat over its outline with the USB-C end towards the board edge. The pads are pulled out past the module, so you can reach them from the side.
  3. Tack two opposite corners: put solder on the iron, touch it to the board pad and the castellation together, and let it flow. Check the module is flat and every castellation sits over its pad. Reheat a corner to adjust it.
  4. Solder the other 12 edge pads the same way: iron on the board pad, solder fed into the castellation. Each joint should be a small concave fillet up the half-hole.
  5. Turn the board over (switch face up). Fill the boxed holes: the pair inside one box (the battery), both holes in the second box (P0.09: one under each module's pad, so fill both), and the round hole in the small square box near the USB end (RST, the module's reset pad). Hold the iron on the ring for 2–3 s and feed solder into the hole until it fills and stops sinking. That means it has wetted the module's pre-tinned pad underneath. Fill only the boxed holes. The unboxed ones belong to the other half. Most sit under bare module areas; the unboxed reset hole sits under the module's GND test pad, and only wires to the other half's (empty) button pads, so it does no harm either way.
  6. Clean off the flux.

The battery holes and one P0.09 hole are smaller than the rest (0.6 mm, as large as the pads above them allow): flux them well, use thin solder (0.5 mm), and hold the iron on the ring until the solder sinks into the hole before you feed more.

Check: - Before any battery goes in: the power switch's middle pin (VBAT) must not beep to the module's GND castellation. A beep means the wrong selector row, or both: fix it first. - The battery hole the selector made + beeps to the power switch's middle pin. - The RST hole does not beep to GND (the module's reset is pulled up inside the module: a beep means a solder bridge, and the half would sit in reset). - No two adjacent castellations beep to each other.

3. Power switch, MSK-12C02 (parts face)

It goes next to the module's USB-C, with the lever pointing out over the board edge and its two locating pegs in the small holes.

  1. Tin one of the four corner (shield) pads. Place the switch with tweezers and reheat that pad to tack it.
  2. Solder the other three shield pads.
  3. Solder the three signal pads with the fine tip and very little solder. A bridge between the middle pin and its near neighbour does no harm (both are VBAT). A bridge from the middle pin to the far one bypasses the switch: wick it away.

4. JST battery socket (parts face)

Its opening faces the battery's spot under the inner keys. Tack one signal pin, check it sits flat, and solder the other pin. Then solder the two large mounting tabs: they need more heat and more solder, because they take the force of plugging the battery in.

5. Hot-swap sockets (parts face)

Twelve, one per key. Each socket fits its outline only one way round.

  1. Tin one pad lightly, place the socket, and hold it flat with tweezers while you reheat the pad until the socket's metal tab sinks into the solder.
  2. Solder the second tab. Use enough solder to make a fillet, but keep it out of the socket's opening.

6. Reset button, TS-1187A (switch face)

It goes on the switch face, in the gap above the thumb keys, inside its outline labelled RST. The part is square and symmetric: any of the four ways round works.

  1. Tin one corner pad, hold the button flat and centred with tweezers, and reheat the pad to tack it.
  2. Solder the other three legs: a small fillet on each, iron on the pad and against the leg. Keep the solder off the body: the button is 2.0 mm tall and the plate's pocket over it leaves only 0.3 mm above the collar.

Check: the RST hole (step 2.4) beeps to the two legs on the RST side of the button (the side with the label), and beeps to GND only while you press the button.

7. Test (before the case)

  1. Plug in USB. With the firmware flashed and your computer paired or connected, short each socket's two contacts with tweezers. Every key should register.
  2. Plug in the battery, unplug USB and toggle the power switch: the half should stay connected.
  3. Press the reset button once: the half reboots (it disconnects and reconnects). Press it twice quickly: a USB drive appears (the bootloader), as with the module's own button.

8. Case and plate

The base is solid up to the PCB's underside, with a pocket for every part. The PCB rests on plastic everywhere, which makes the half stiff and quiet. The top plate covers the whole board: the switches clip into it, and a shallow pocket in its underside clears the solder around the module.

  1. Fit 13 common 8 × 1.5 mm N52 discs per half (polarity!): seven underneath and six along the seam. Dry-fit first, without forcing the plastic, then apply a little suitable adhesive. Keep adhesive off the mating faces.
  2. Lay the battery in its bay and plug it into the JST socket. The plug and the slack of its leads sit in the bay, between the battery and the socket.
  3. Lower the PCB, parts face down, into the base. Each part drops into its pocket, the switch lever slides down its slot, and the USB-C port lines up with its recess. Nothing should need force: every pocket has 0.6 mm of clearance, and the PCB 0.5 mm to the outer walls.
  4. Lay the plate on top, its pillars and screw bosses resting on the PCB, and fit the four M3 × 8 flat-head screws through the plate and PCB. They cut their own thread in the Nylon: turn them in slowly, and stop when the heads sit flush in their countersinks.
  5. Clip the switches through the plate into the sockets, then fit the keycaps. The reset button now sits under a cone in the plate, between the index finger's bottom key and the thumbs. Its plunger is 0.2 mm under the plate's top, so keycaps and palms don't reach it.
  6. Anti-slip pads. Each floor has five slots, each long enough for two pads. Stick one 8 × 2 mm bump-on into one end of every slot and leave the other end empty: when the halves are stacked back to back, the other half's pad drops into that empty end, so the floors and magnets still meet.

The rule is the same on both halves: turn the half over and go clockwise round the floor. In every slot, the pad goes in the end you reach second. Then each half's five pads sit in the ends the other half leaves empty.

Clean the slots with isopropyl alcohol first and press each pad down firmly against the slot's roof, centred in its round end. Stack the halves to check: they should close flat, with no rocking.

Optional stands and drawer retention

  • Use the same 8 × 1.5 mm N52 discs everywhere. The dock takes six wing magnets and six side magnets per half and five base pairs (including five glued to the Ugreen base): 34 discs total. No rear magnet is fitted; leave USB and cable clearance unobstructed.
  • Each insert takes three evenly spaced side magnets per side (mating seam positions 2, 4 and 6). The halves and dock have six per side at 17.8 mm pitch; the inserts use every other position, at 35.6 mm pitch. Check the full half → dock → insert polarity chain before gluing.
  • Trackball: secure the wired nice!nano face down on the shell ledges, not on the floor. Leave slack to lower the battery/sensor floor independently after removing its three screws. The trackpad controller still moves with its floor. Disconnect wiring before full separation; the renders labelled “Cutaway” do not illustrate disassembly.
  • Trackball printing: the supplied top-down MX/Choc shells need build-plate supports beneath the projecting rear hood (4.0/1.2 mm bed gap). Paint supports across its underside, keep ball/switch apertures clear, inspect the first hood layers and removal access in the slicer, and test-print the hood first. Support settings and removal remain physically unvalidated. Print floors flat; their foot recesses retain a modeled 0.7 mm roof.
  • Test front pull-out removal with the dock held down. The insert slides horizontally, without lifting, front notches or protruding handles. Verify retention with real parts before carrying or lap use.
  • For USB use while docked, remove the module, feed the USB-C plug through the dock's 12.8 mm upper rear passage in its normal orientation, connect it to the module and slide the module in while feeding the cable. The modeled 12.35 × 6.5 mm overmold fits; actual plugs and bend radii vary. If yours does not fit, charge with the module removed. Unplug before pulling out a module with a restrained cable.
  • The rear top hood and dock support meet at a 45° slope with 0.25 mm modeled normal clearance. Check printed fit and horizontal removal before loading the magnets; do not force the pieces together.
  • For independent tenting, print case/stand_magnet_template_left.svg and case/stand_magnet_template_right.svg at 100%. Check the 54 mm boundary with a ruler. Glue three mates to each stand's tilting phone platform, not its base; the patterns are mirrored and the magnet centres form a 44 mm circle.
  • Measure the platform first: it needs a clear flat 54 mm contact patch. Use 2.0 mm bump-ons; the modeled platform clearance is 0.6 mm. Actual platform fit, adhesive strength and retention remain unverified. Start flat, support the half while increasing tilt, then test typing forces before relying on it. The COG model is an estimate, not a holding-force certification.

Resetting a closed half

Press straight down into the cone above the thumbs with a fingertip (the pad, not the nail, is enough: the cone is 4.2 mm across at the top and the plunger 2.0 mm wide).

  • Once: the half reboots.
  • Twice quickly: the bootloader. With USB plugged in, a USB drive appears: copy a .uf2 onto it to flash new firmware, as in "Before you start".

Printing

  • Base: floor-down, no supports (the wall over the USB recess and the roofs of the pad slots, 8.6 mm wide, bridge). For a quiet, solid base, use 4–5 walls and 40–100% infill (gyroid, or solid). More infill means less hollow sound and more weight.
  • Plate: top face down: the STL is already oriented that way, with the pillars and screw bosses pointing up. The reset button's cone narrows upwards at 45° from the bed (4.2 mm to 2.8 mm over the first 0.7 mm) and needs no support. Print it solid, at 0.2 mm layers. The switch cutouts are 14.0 mm (13.8 mm + 0.1 mm per side); if your switches are loose or tight, change cut_tol in design.py and regenerate.
  • Nylon: dry the filament before printing. It's tough enough to take the self-tapping threads without cracking, and its softness damps key noise.
